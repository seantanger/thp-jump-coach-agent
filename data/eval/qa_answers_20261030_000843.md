# Private Coach QA answers

## q01: The tendon under my kneecap aches after jumping. Do isometric holds actually help, and how should I do them?

Yes, according to the library. Isometrics are the starting point for patellar tendon pain. Do straight-leg knee extension holds at a moderate load, for 30–45 seconds, every day. Progress by adding load or by moving the knee angle lower. Stop short of the point where the leg shakes. Eccentric work and decline boards are not better supported.

- Isometrics are the first step to get the tendon pain-free and start rebuilding strength. Do them every day, because an unloaded tendon gets softer and weaker. [THP Strength – Isometrics for Patellar Tendinopathy: Why Load and Sets Matter @ 01:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U9UECfyq3uI&t=109s) [THP Strength – Isometrics for Patellar Tendinopathy: Why Load and Sets Matter @ 00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U9UECfyq3uI&t=0s)

- The reasons given are that isometrics load the tendon with almost no compression and no velocity, and you can control the load. They are also said to calm nervous-system inhibition and, when held long enough, to signal collagen production. One coach compares them to 'ibuprofen for your knees', saying pain should drop quickly. [THP Strength – Fix Knee Pain for Jumpers With One Isometric Exercise @ 03:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J4uDvYzlh_4&t=193s) [THP Strength – 10-Minute Routine to Fix Knee Pain and Jump Higher @ 00:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuGRrf_RjUE&t=36s)

- The most effective version for knee pain is a straight-leg knee extension hold. Use a leg extension machine or a kettlebell at your foot. Hold the leg out just before lockout, at about 20–30 degrees of flexion. If your morning pain is above 3/10, start these. [THP Strength – The Exact Protocol to Fix Knee Pain and Get Back to Jumping @ 00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VMSF8C25mxY&t=0s)

- Other options that load the quads are a Spanish squat, a wall sit, a single-leg wall sit, a quarter pistol squat hold, or a manual hold where you push against your hands. [THP Strength – 10-Minute Routine to Fix Knee Pain and Jump Higher @ 00:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuGRrf_RjUE&t=36s) [THP Strength – Knee Pain Killing Your Vertical? Start With Isometrics @ 00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TMznIxcRJGg&t=0s)

- Hold for 30–45 seconds. The intensity is right if you feel you could only hold about 15 more seconds at the end of the set. Knee isometrics use a lighter load and longer holds than max-effort isometrics, which are 2–5 seconds. [THP Strength – 10-Minute Routine to Fix Knee Pain and Jump Higher @ 00:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuGRrf_RjUE&t=36s) [THP Strength – Improve Elasticity To Jump Higher With MAX Isometrics (Progressions Included) @ 02:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UoymcKL9_Lg&t=173s)

- To progress, increase the load. Try to stay just short of the point where the leg shakes or vibrates, because at that point it is no longer truly isometric. You can also move the angle so the leg goes from fully extended toward more bend, which changes the tendon load. The speaker says tendon load is dictated by angle. [THP Strength – Isometrics for Patellar Tendinopathy: Why Load and Sets Matter @ 03:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U9UECfyq3uI&t=224s) [THP Strength – Isometrics for Patellar Tendinopathy: Why Load and Sets Matter @ 04:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U9UECfyq3uI&t=260s)

- If you are still competing, make the holds part of your warm-up. The tendon needs load every day, and the loading will be cycled later. [THP Strength – Isometrics for Patellar Tendinopathy: Why Load and Sets Matter @ 04:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U9UECfyq3uI&t=296s)

- The speaker says the support for eccentrics and decline boards is not very good. Eccentrics can load the tendon more than the person can handle. [THP Strength – Isometrics for Patellar Tendinopathy: Why Load and Sets Matter @ 00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U9UECfyq3uI&t=0s) [THP Strength – Isometrics for Patellar Tendinopathy: Why Load and Sets Matter @ 01:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U9UECfyq3uI&t=109s)

Do daily straight-leg knee extension isometrics at a moderate load. Hold for 30–45 seconds, stop before the leg shakes, and add load gradually.

See a physio if pain is sharp/stabbing or steadily worsening, occurs at rest or wakes you at night, lasts over two weeks without improving despite cutting load, or follows a fall/impact; or if you have swelling, bruising, deformity, a pop, giving way, locking, catching, numbness, tingling, radiating weakness or limping. Back pain with bladder/bowel changes or groin numbness needs emergency care.

## q02: How much knee pain is acceptable during a jump session before I should stop, and how do I know if I overdid it?

Library guidance on activity-related tendon pain: a little pain is tolerable if it settles within 24–48 hours and trends down week to week. A consistent next-morning pain test shows whether you overdid it. Pain that is sharp, worsening, or lasts more than a couple of weeks is a reason to see a physio.

- A little pain is okay as long as it isn't lingering beyond 24–48 hours. You may feel a bit more the next day, but if it's gone by 48 hours you're probably fine. Across jump sessions, pain should decrease from one week to the next. If it doesn't, you tolerated too much pain. [John Evans – Knee Mistakes That Kill Your Vertical Jump Gains @ 01:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1qDymLUveIg&t=111s)

- Tendons respond late to load. Pain may not show during the session and tends to peak 24–48 hours afterwards. Judge a session by how you feel the next morning, not just how you feel during it. [THP Strength – How to Fix Patellar Tendon Pain Without Quitting Your Sport @ 03:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZN8wh4FFZuA&t=187s) [THP Strength – Set a Strict Time Cap on Your Jump Sessions and Watch What Happens @ 03:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ChOpvLxfZZc&t=215s)

- Use a consistent morning test to check whether you overdid it. One example is a deep bodyweight squat, scoring pain from 1 to 10. Keep this morning pain under about 3/10. Jumps in a rehab or return phase should be low effort, and elastic work should ideally feel pain-free. [THP Strength – How to Fix Patellar Tendon Pain Without Quitting Your Sport @ 03:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZN8wh4FFZuA&t=187s) [THP Strength – How to Fix Patellar Tendon Pain Without Quitting Your Sport @ 03:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZN8wh4FFZuA&t=222s)

- Cap session length with a timer and stop when it goes off, even if you feel great. The source says stopping because you feel a little knee pain is the wrong time, since it will feel worse the next day. [THP Strength – Set a Strict Time Cap on Your Jump Sessions and Watch What Happens @ 03:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ChOpvLxfZZc&t=215s)

- If pain comes back 24–48 hours after jumping or other elastic loading, that points to tendon disrepair rather than a simple reactive flare. A reactive flare often settles with 24–48 hours off, but it can progress if you keep jumping at the level that caused it. [John Evans – How To Get Rid Of Knee Pain (Full Blueprint) @ 01:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9X_j-oF2ZDg&t=83s)

- If you push through and the tendon problem lingers, it can start to limit your performance. In that case, move to a load-management setup that still includes progressive training. [John Evans – What do I do if I've tried load management and my knee pain comes back? Day 52 @ 02:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TEQsK1qSKhg&t=122s)

Mild pain that settles within 48 hours and trends down week to week is generally acceptable. Check with a consistent next-morning pain test, and keep sessions short and capped. Pain that is higher the next morning, lingers, or isn't improving week to week means you did too much.

See a physio if pain is sharp/stabbing or steadily worsening, occurs at rest or wakes you at night, lasts over two weeks without improving despite cutting load, or follows a fall/impact; or if you have swelling, bruising, deformity, a pop, giving way, locking, catching, numbness, tingling, radiating weakness or limping. Back pain with bladder/bowel changes or groin numbness needs emergency care.

## q03: My Achilles has been sore for months. What exercises should I start with to rehab it?

Start with daily isometric calf holds at a load that doesn't aggravate the tendon. Then add heavy, slow calf strength work every other day, and only later add drops and jumping. Because your Achilles has been sore for months, see a physio if it isn't improving.

- Step 1 is isometrics. Pick a calf exercise that loads the calf and Achilles, hold it for about 45 seconds at a weight that doesn't hurt, and stay out of full dorsiflexion (the compressed position). Do this daily. [THP Strength – The Best Calf Raise Progression To Jump Higher (Advanced Athletes Only) @ 06:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wgKybvDwYZo&t=368s) [THP Strength – The Best Calf Raise Progression To Jump Higher (Advanced Athletes Only) @ 06:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wgKybvDwYZo&t=412s)

- Use five sets. If the load is heavy enough, your pain should drop by about half across the sets (for example from a 7 to a 3–4). If it doesn't, add external load such as a dumbbell, seated calf raise, standing calf raise machine or barbell. [John Evans – The Exact System To Cure Achilles Tendon Pain @ 08:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DXCZXnaF3PM&t=529s) [John Evans – The Exact System To Cure Achilles Tendon Pain @ 09:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DXCZXnaF3PM&t=566s)

- Step 2 is heavy slow strength work for the calf, added every other day on top of the isometrics. Calf raises are the example given. You can go single-leg, or use a metronome tempo of 4 seconds up and 4 seconds down. [THP Strength – The Best Calf Raise Progression To Jump Higher (Advanced Athletes Only) @ 06:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wgKybvDwYZo&t=412s) [THP Strength – How to Rehab Back Pain and Tendon Issues Without Losing Your Vertical @ 02:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xcmVCG2F2E0&t=159s) [THP Strength – How to Actually Rehab Injuries @ 16:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CuANQSa5Mj0&t=1019s)

- Don't go back to jumping as soon as the pain is gone. Build calf strength first. Then, about 3–4 weeks later, add drops (stepping off a box and landing) to teach the tendon to store energy elastically. After that, progress to countermovement jumps. [THP Strength – How to Rehab Back Pain and Tendon Issues Without Losing Your Vertical @ 02:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xcmVCG2F2E0&t=159s) [John Evans – The Exact System To Cure Achilles Tendon Pain @ 13:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DXCZXnaF3PM&t=821s)

- Check how the tendon feels after drops. If pain goes up, lower the box height and return to heavy slow strength work until you're stronger. [John Evans – The Exact System To Cure Achilles Tendon Pain @ 13:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DXCZXnaF3PM&t=821s)

Progress in this order: isometrics, then heavy slow calf strength, then drops, then jumps.

See a physio if pain is sharp/stabbing or steadily worsening, occurs at rest or wakes you at night, lasts over two weeks without improving despite cutting load, or follows a fall/impact; or if you have swelling, bruising, deformity, a pop, giving way, locking, catching, numbness, tingling, radiating weakness or limping. Back pain with bladder/bowel changes or groin numbness needs emergency care.

## q04: Should I just ice my sore knee, take anti-inflammatories and rest it for a couple of weeks?

The library doesn't back a plan of ice, anti-inflammatories and two weeks of rest. For activity-related knee tendon ache, it recommends limiting icing and avoiding anti-inflammatories. It also recommends loading the tendon with isometrics instead of complete rest. Pain that doesn't improve over about two weeks despite cutting load needs a physio or doctor.

- In a knee-pain coaching session, the coach asked about icing and anti-inflammatories. He said to limit icing as much as possible. The athlete wasn't taking anti-inflammatories, and the coach called that good. [THP Strength – Helping A Volleyball Player With His Knee Pain @ 09:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ix92ivfHgmY&t=573s)

- Stopping the aggravating activity is only the first step. Resting and then doing strength work is what gets a tendon better. Loading the tendon will make it better. [THP Strength – Isometrics for Patellar Tendinopathy: Why Load and Sets Matter @ 00:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U9UECfyq3uI&t=38s) [THP Strength – Isometrics for Patellar Tendinopathy: Why Load and Sets Matter @ 01:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U9UECfyq3uI&t=70s)

- The library describes isometrics as 'like ibuprofen for your knees'. It says they calm the nervous system, and pain should drop after 3–5 sets. Hold each for 30–45 seconds at a load where you could only hold about 15 more seconds. [THP Strength – 10-Minute Routine to Fix Knee Pain and Jump Higher @ 00:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuGRrf_RjUE&t=36s) [THP Strength – 10-Minute Routine to Fix Knee Pain and Jump Higher @ 01:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuGRrf_RjUE&t=74s)

- On days without heavy lifting or jumping, one source suggests 3–5 sets, three times a day, spaced about 6 hours apart. [THP Strength – Knee Pain Killing Your Vertical? Start With Isometrics @ 02:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TMznIxcRJGg&t=137s)

- Another source suggests 5 sets of 45 seconds at about 70% load with 2 minutes' rest. It says that if pain doesn't drop, you either didn't hold long enough or didn't go heavy enough. [THP Strength – Isometrics for Patellar Tendinopathy: Why Load and Sets Matter @ 02:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U9UECfyq3uI&t=143s)

- Tendon pain tracks load and speed. Jumping hurts the most, and slow squats hurt less. Pain right at the tendon just below the kneecap suggests a patellar tendon problem. [John Evans – What Is Load Management? @ 18:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A7PWEXAgbNI&t=1120s)

- Loading too aggressively, such as with a decline board or high-volume eccentrics, can make a tendon worse. The load has to match what the tendon can currently handle. [THP Strength – Isometrics for Patellar Tendinopathy: Why Load and Sets Matter @ 00:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U9UECfyq3uI&t=38s) [THP Strength – Isometrics for Patellar Tendinopathy: Why Load and Sets Matter @ 01:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U9UECfyq3uI&t=70s)

Don't default to ice, anti-inflammatories and total rest. Cut back what provokes the pain, and use properly dosed isometrics to calm it down and rebuild tendon capacity.

## q05: How often should I take an easy week, and should I just do nothing during it?

Take a deload about every 3–4 weeks. Don't do nothing during it. Cut your volume and keep some high-intensity work.

- Deload every 3–4 weeks so you can keep quality sessions and don't build up too much fatigue. A quality session during the deload week is typical. [THP Strength – Do These 5 Things if Your Vertical is STUCK @ 06:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JEt6lYqcnjE&t=365s)

- For a very taxing cycle, deload around week 3 or 4. Don't push it much further out, because pushing too hard for too long builds up a 'neuromuscular debt'. [THP Strength – How Many Days A Week Should You Train Explosively To Jump Higher? @ 04:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_C8nl0YN5M4&t=280s)

- A common mistake is treating a deload as doing nothing for two to three weeks, which can make your vertical go down. Keep doing max-effort jumping. [John Evans – How to Add Inches to Your Vertical So Fast It Feels Like Cheating @ 08:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6P6ONA5ELyA&t=501s)

- The opposite mistake is turning the deload into extra work. Piling on lots of jumps or hard cleans can raise your volume by 100–200%, and then it isn't a deload. [John Evans – How to Add Inches to Your Vertical So Fast It Feels Like Cheating @ 08:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6P6ONA5ELyA&t=501s)

- When you unload, drop volume by roughly 50–70%, depending on how fatigued you are. Keep intensity high with a little sprinting, plyos, heavyish squats or power cleans to stay sharp. [John Evans – You Won't Jump Higher If You Don't Understand This (I Promise You're Doing It Wrong) @ 04:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bFezqBUmWO4&t=242s)

Deload every 3–4 weeks by cutting volume substantially, not by stopping. Keep a small amount of high-intensity work.

## q06: Is it better to spend a few months only on strength, then switch to power, then plyos, or train everything at the same time?

The library doesn't favour pure strength-then-power-then-plyos blocks, and it doesn't favour training everything equally at once. THP calls the strict-sequence model outdated. They prefer a long conjugate approach. You train all qualities, emphasise one for each month-long block, and keep the others at a lower volume. John Evans describes a sequential, block-style approach in which one quality potentiates the next. He still keeps strength work in the mix.

- Sequential periodization means focusing on one quality at a time, for example max strength in month one, power in month two and elasticity in month three. THP describes this as an outdated way to view training. [THP Strength – Sequential vs Concurrent vs Conjugate Periodization Explained @ 00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dUFz438g4Kc&t=0s)

- Concurrent periodization trains all qualities at once with equal volume. The downside is injury risk, because the volume of the other qualities is never reduced. [THP Strength – Sequential vs Concurrent vs Conjugate Periodization Explained @ 01:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dUFz438g4Kc&t=95s) [THP Strength – Sequential vs Concurrent vs Conjugate Periodization Explained @ 00:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dUFz438g4Kc&t=48s)

- Conjugate periodization is similar to concurrent. You emphasise one quality and lower the emphasis on the other two, so you still train all of them and retain them. THP uses long conjugate-sequence systems, where the emphasis shifts from one mesocycle to the next. [THP Strength – Sequential vs Concurrent vs Conjugate Periodization Explained @ 01:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dUFz438g4Kc&t=95s) [THP Strength – Sequential vs Concurrent vs Conjugate Periodization Explained @ 02:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dUFz438g4Kc&t=136s)

- John Evans uses sequential (block) periodization. He focuses on one physical quality for a period and uses it to potentiate the next. He bases the emphasis on needs analysis, such as force-plate data. Low peak force points to max strength. Low rate of force development points to power or reactive work. Strength is still worked in both cases. [John Evans – The Only Way I've Been Able To Coach 5 Athletes To A 50" Vertical @ 05:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UB2qCPnHCwU&t=334s)

- THP says the best coaches in the world for speed and power do not use true block periodization, which means doing one thing and then another. Even the Russian program they tested only came close to block periodization. [THP Strength – We're Testing A Russian Plyometric Program @ 01:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JoXfKavjn8g&t=93s)

- A simple practical template from THP has sprinting, cleans and strength sessions three times a week, plus one jump session. You repeat it for 3 weeks, deload in week 4, then repeat the cycle. This keeps jumping in the programme throughout. [THP Strength – How to Train Plyometrics for a Higher Vertical (Full Breakdown) @ 02:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NeXMYJ-j-jw&t=160s)

Don't drop everything except one quality, and don't train everything equally. Emphasise one quality for each block and keep small doses of the others. Choose the emphasis based on your weakest area.

## q07: How many high-intensity training days per week can I handle if I want to jump higher?

## q10: I'm a two-foot jumper. Will depth jumps carry over to my vertical?

For a two-foot jumper, standard fast-contact depth jumps usually carry over poorly. Two-foot jumpers get more from plyos with bigger ranges of motion, from loaded jumps and from acceleration work. Depth jumps can still be made more specific by raising the drop so the eccentric forces are very high.

- Depth jumps aim for very short ground contact and high reactivity. The best at them tend to be sprinters and one-foot jumpers, and the lift comes mainly from the lower leg (Achilles, gastroc and soleus). [THP Strength – Why Depth Jumps Don't Transfer to Two Foot Jumpers @ 00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vqqjLNobS9Y&t=0s) [THP Strength – Why Depth Jumps Don't Transfer to Two Foot Jumpers @ 01:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vqqjLNobS9Y&t=75s)

- Two-foot jumps have ground contact times of about 240–300 ms, much longer than stiff depth jumps or hops. The patella and quad also contribute energy storage during that time, so the two jump types load the body differently. [THP Strength – Why Depth Jumps Don't Transfer to Two Foot Jumpers @ 01:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vqqjLNobS9Y&t=75s)

- Depth jumps work for one-foot jumpers because the patellar tendon is stretched almost immediately as the takeoff foot goes out in front. Lots of very stiff, ankle-dominant contacts will not move a two-foot vertical. [THP Strength – Why Two-Foot Jumpers Need More Strength Than One-Foot Jumpers @ 00:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8sBB2jAg_00&t=37s)

- The coach's own experience matches this. Years of RSI-focused plyos and one-foot work gave him a great RSI but a lower two-foot vertical. His two-foot vertical rose quickly once he got much stronger, practiced two-foot jumps and changed the type of plyos he did. [THP Strength – Depth Jumps Not Helping Your Two-Foot Vertical? Here's Why @ 01:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XEbE_GW9ORM&t=115s)

- For two-foot jumpers, single-response or multi-response jumps with bigger ranges of motion are more specific than the most intense, stiff plyos. Dunking and max approach jumps are the most specific plyo of all. [THP Strength – How to Progress Plyos From Phase Zero to Shock Loading @ 04:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MZe_rHoGh3E&t=255s)

- If you want depth-jump-style work, raise the eccentric forces enough to push you into the range of motion of a two-foot jump. He used a 60-inch box for this because it made him sink into a half squat instead of a quarter. Sprint acceleration (the first three steps) and loaded jumps are also very specific for two-footers. [THP Strength – How to Progress Plyos From Phase Zero to Shock Loading @ 04:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MZe_rHoGh3E&t=255s) [THP Strength – How to Progress Plyos From Phase Zero to Shock Loading @ 04:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MZe_rHoGh3E&t=288s)

Fast, stiff depth jumps mostly build ankle stiffness, which is more useful to one-foot jumpers. As a two-footer, put more weight on deeper-range plyos, high-drop depth jumps that force a deeper squat, loaded jumps and acceleration work.

## q11: My reactive strength score keeps going up but my vertical doesn't. Is that number a good way to track progress?

RSI can rise without your vertical rising. It's flight time divided by ground contact time, so a shorter ground contact time raises it even if you don't jump higher. It works better as a supporting number than as your main progress metric. Max-effort jump height, especially on an approach jump, is the better thing to track.

- RSI is flight time divided by ground contact time. You can raise it by jumping higher or by cutting ground contact time. You can therefore score higher without producing more propulsive force or jumping higher. [John Evans – Why This Metric Has Nothing To Do With How High You Jump @ 05:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OjOw3T_sCn0&t=300s) [John Evans – Why This Metric Has Nothing To Do With How High You Jump @ 00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OjOw3T_sCn0&t=0s)

- The way you do the test changes the score a lot. Isaiah's RSI rose when he focused on minimizing ground contact time. When he went purely for max height, his RSI was lowest and his ground contact time was longest. [THP Strength – You Aren't Doing Things Fast Enough To Jump Higher @ 04:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BAUJEIq9bj0&t=252s)

- Evans says that if RSI is your base metric, you can spend months chasing a number that doesn't show how well you use elastic energy. Getting better at the test, meaning lower ground contact times, doesn't guarantee a higher jump. [John Evans – Why This Metric Has Nothing To Do With How High You Jump @ 00:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OjOw3T_sCn0&t=55s)

- The coaches prefer to measure it on a max approach jump. You aren't trying to maximize RSI there. You just jump as high as possible and watch how that number changes over time. They say the repeat-hop test is easier to game with technique and may not transfer as well to training. [THP Strength – Improving This Could Get You Jumping Higher FAST @ 03:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pGW46ON4Wgc&t=229s)

- Ground contact time still matters as a diagnostic. If someone jumps high but has long ground contact times, that points to a deficit in storing and releasing energy quickly. Contact times should trend down as the vertical climbs. [THP Strength – Improving This Could Get You Jumping Higher FAST @ 07:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pGW46ON4Wgc&t=422s)

- Evans is increasingly interested in the coefficient of reactivity, which is how high you jump relative to how far you fell. His benchmark is that your drop-jump ground contact time should be within 100 ms of the contact time you use in your real jump. That way you can't cheat the test by landing, pausing and doing a countermovement jump. [John Evans – Why This Metric Has Nothing To Do With How High You Jump @ 10:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OjOw3T_sCn0&t=633s)

Track your max-effort jump height, preferably an approach jump. Use RSI and ground contact time only as supporting diagnostics, and make sure the RSI test isn't being gamed with a short-contact strategy.

## q23: Mechanically, how is sprinting different from jumping?

The main differences are the direction of force, ground contact time, braking forces, joint positions, limb speed and the number of phases. The two movements also share some features: both use the triple extensors and the stretch-shortening cycle. One source argues they are essentially the same movement turned sideways.

- Direction and goal differ. A vertical jump converts horizontal momentum into vertical velocity or momentum. Sprinting aims to generate as much horizontal velocity as possible. [THP Strength – Should You Train Like A Sprinter To Jump Higher? @ 01:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yk1A9eYD8dg&t=86s) [THP Strength – Should You Train Like A Sprinter To Jump Higher? @ 02:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yk1A9eYD8dg&t=126s)

- Ground contact time and force differ. Jumping has relatively longer ground contacts and higher forces. Sprinting has very short ground contacts, and it adds stride frequency and limb speed as factors. [THP Strength – Should You Train Like A Sprinter To Jump Higher? @ 01:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yk1A9eYD8dg&t=86s)

- Braking differs. In a jump you get a very high horizontal and vertical braking force at foot contact, and you use it to maximise the stretch-shortening cycle and elastic return. In sprinting the vertical braking forces are considerably lower and horizontal braking is minimised. [THP Strength – Should You Train Like A Sprinter To Jump Higher? @ 02:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yk1A9eYD8dg&t=126s) [THP Strength – Should You Train Like A Sprinter To Jump Higher? @ 02:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yk1A9eYD8dg&t=167s)

- Impulse shape differs. Jumps want a very high, maximised impulse. Sprinting wants an idealised impulse. [THP Strength – Should You Train Like A Sprinter To Jump Higher? @ 03:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yk1A9eYD8dg&t=203s)

- Positions and limb speeds differ. Limbs move much faster in sprinting. Top-speed sprinting posture is roughly an eighth-squat depth or shallower, and acceleration is perhaps around a third squat. This was described as a ballpark estimate. [THP Strength – Should You Train Like A Sprinter To Jump Higher? @ 03:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yk1A9eYD8dg&t=203s)

- Phase structure differs. A jump has essentially one takeoff, with the run-up being less important. Sprinting has multiple phases, and every step matters. [THP Strength – Should You Train Like A Sprinter To Jump Higher? @ 02:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yk1A9eYD8dg&t=167s) [THP Strength – Should You Train Like A Sprinter To Jump Higher? @ 03:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yk1A9eYD8dg&t=203s)

- They share features. Both use the triple extensors and the stretch-shortening cycle at very high levels, and both have vertical ground reaction forces at foot contact. [THP Strength – Should You Train Like A Sprinter To Jump Higher? @ 02:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yk1A9eYD8dg&t=167s)

- One view is that sprinting and jumping are the same thing turned sideways. Ground contact times shorten and forces rise as a sprinter accelerates toward max velocity. The plant-leg positions at block clearance resemble two-foot jumping. [THP Strength – Can You Focus On Multiple Different Sprint Qualities During The Same Cycle? @ 10:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ddRnDa6IsV0&t=628s)

Sprinting and jumping both use the stretch-shortening cycle and triple extension. They differ in force direction, contact time, braking, impulse shape, limb speed and phase structure.

## q30: I gained a lot in my first year but now I barely improve. Is that normal or am I doing something wrong?

Slower progress after a big first year is normal, because adaptation slows as you become more adapted. It doesn't prove you're doing something wrong. Judge a plateau over a long window, and if you really are stuck, check whether your training applies progressive overload and varies the emphasis over time.

- Slower gains after the beginner stage are expected. Adaptation happens more slowly as you become more adapted, which is called diminishing returns. Each step up in training level makes improving in a given timeline harder. [THP Strength – How Much Vertical You Should Expect Each Year of Jump Training @ 02:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qkjAT0fKh1Y&t=139s)

- The law of accommodation says the body eventually stops adapting to the same stimulus. Weights, reps, sets and so on need to progress over time, and you have to keep giving the body a reason to adapt. [THP Strength – How to Break a Vertical Jump Plateau Using Periodization @ 03:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QBZEP9cN7Zs&t=196s)

- Don't judge progress over a few weeks. With 3 or more years of jump training, compare today to the same day last year. If you haven't improved over that span, you are plateaued. How you jump now reflects the last few months of training, not the last few weeks. [THP Strength – How to Break Through Any Vertical Jump Plateau (My Exact System) @ 02:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NKfbeopcspg&t=126s)

- A plateau depends on the time window you look at. In the short term you may look stuck, but over a longer window you may have progressed a lot. [THP Strength – How to Break Through Any Vertical Jump Plateau (My Exact System) @ 00:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NKfbeopcspg&t=46s)

- If you are plateaued, check whether your training applied the key training principles. One suggested approach is long conjugate-sequence periodization. This means training several qualities at once, such as strength, speed and rate of force development, with extra emphasis on one at a time. That way you don't detrain the others. [THP Strength – How to Break a Vertical Jump Plateau Using Periodization @ 05:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QBZEP9cN7Zs&t=306s)

- What to emphasize depends on the athlete. A strong person who doesn't feel bouncy has an elastic deficit and might start with high-specificity work, then go to max strength, then return to high specificity before a taper. Someone with poor jump technique may need to jump more often. [THP Strength – The Taper Secret Behind Austin's Biggest Vertical PRs @ 01:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NQExJEOcPzo&t=88s)

- Injuries tend to appear at more advanced levels, so it matters to manage training carefully. [THP Strength – How Much Vertical You Should Expect Each Year of Jump Training @ 02:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qkjAT0fKh1Y&t=139s)

Slower progress is normal. Judge it over months or a year, and if you are truly stuck, change how you periodize your training.
